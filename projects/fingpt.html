Fin GPT: Financial Research RAG Over Two Decades of Annual Reports

A research assistant built over a client's annual-report archive — twenty-plus years of filings where the answers live in charts, dense tables and diagrams rather than clean prose. The system reads all of it: text, tables, charts and images are ingested into one index, queries are rewritten before they are embedded, retrieval runs lexically and semantically at the same time, and the conversation remembers what came before.

The Problem

The answer is in the chart, not the paragraph

An analyst asks a simple-sounding question: how has the dividend payout ratio moved over the last fifteen years, and what did management say about it each time? Answering that means opening twenty-odd PDFs, finding the right note in each one, reading a table, and cross-checking a bar chart in the business review. It is an afternoon's work, repeated every time the question changes slightly.

Building a retrieval system over that archive sounds like a solved problem until you look at the source documents. An annual report is not an article. Its most valuable content is deliberately not prose: five-year summary tables, segment breakdowns, waterfall charts, ratio grids, org diagrams, footnote-heavy notes to the accounts. A conventional text-only pipeline — extract text, split every 1,000 characters, embed, retrieve — throws away or mangles exactly the parts an analyst came for.

Three failure modes of text-only RAG on filings Tables collapse into a soup of unlabelled numbers when flattened to plain text. Charts vanish entirely, because they are images. And a naive character splitter happily cuts a five-year summary in half, so the retrieved chunk has the 2019 column and not its header row.

Where the answers actually live

Before writing any code I catalogued what a question tends to depend on. Four content types matter, and each needs different handling on the way into the index:

Narrative text

Chairman's statement, business review, risk section, management commentary. The only part conventional RAG handles well — and the part analysts trust least on its own.

Tables

Financial statements, notes, segment and five-year summaries. Meaningful only if the header row, units and currency stay attached to the values.

Charts & graphs

Revenue bridges, margin trends, volume mixes. Invisible to a text extractor, and frequently the only place a particular series is published.

Diagrams & images

Group structures, operating-model diagrams, plant maps. Context that explains why the numbers move the way they do.

What the system had to do

  • Index every modality — a chart the analyst can see should be a chart the retriever can find
  • Never break a table — a retrieved table fragment without its headers is worse than no result
  • Bridge the vocabulary gap — analysts ask short questions; filings answer in long, formal, standardised language
  • Find exact figures and fuzzy concepts — "note 14" and "how did management justify it" need different retrieval behaviour
  • Know which year it is talking about — with twenty years of near-identical documents, metadata is not optional
  • Hold a conversation — "and the year after?" has to work, both within a session and across them
Design principle Every number in an answer must be traceable back to a specific chunk, from a specific year, on a specific page. A financial research tool that cannot show its working is not usable by the people who need it.
01 · Ingestion

Multimodal ingestion: reading the whole document

The ingestion pipeline treats a PDF as a mixed-media object rather than a bag of text. Each report goes through conversion, is split into a text stream and an image stream, and the two streams are processed differently before being reunited in a single index.

Conversion: Marker and Opendataloader

Marker and Opendataloader do the heavy lifting of turning a filing into structured Markdown. Between them they recover reading order across multi-column layouts, keep heading hierarchy intact, and — critically — emit tables as Markdown tables rather than as a run of loose numbers. The same pass separates out embedded figures, so every chart, graph and diagram comes out as its own image with a page reference.

Figures: a vision-language model writes the caption

An image is useless to a text retriever, so each extracted figure is sent to a vision-language model, which writes a description of what the figure shows: the chart type, the axes and units, the series plotted, the direction of travel, and any values printed on the figure itself. That description is indexed as a chunk in its own right, carrying a pointer back to the original image and page.

Why this matters more than it sounds Once a chart has a written description, a question like "which segment's margin fell fastest after 2015?" can match a bar chart, not just the paragraph next to it. The index stops being a text index and becomes a description of the whole document.

Structure-preserving chunking

Chunking is where most RAG pipelines quietly lose their accuracy. Two chunkers run side by side:

  • Hierarchical chunking for narrative content — the document's own heading tree defines the boundaries, so a chunk is a section or subsection rather than an arbitrary character window, and each chunk carries its ancestry as context.
  • Table-aware chunking via the Chunkie library for anything tabular — a table is either kept whole or split on row boundaries with its header row replicated into every part. A table is never cut in half.

Everything is then enriched with metadata before indexing: reporting year, report type, section path, page number and modality. That metadata is what makes "compare 2011 with 2019" answerable rather than a lucky guess.

ingest.py
# ── Multimodal ingestion: one report in, many chunks out ─────────────
from marker.converters.pdf import PdfConverter
from marker.models import create_model_dict
from chunkie import TableChunker, RecursiveChunker

converter = PdfConverter(artifact_dict=create_model_dict())

def ingest_report(pdf_path, year, report_type):
    # 1. PDF -> markdown text (+ tables) and a set of extracted figures
    rendered = converter(pdf_path)
    markdown, _, images = text_from_rendered(rendered)

    chunks = []

    # 2. Narrative + tables: hierarchical, table-aware chunking
    for block in split_by_heading_tree(markdown):
        chunker = TableChunker() if block.is_table else RecursiveChunker(
            chunk_size=900, chunk_overlap=120
        )
        for piece in chunker.chunk(block.content):
            chunks.append({
                'text':     piece.text,
                'modality': 'table' if block.is_table else 'text',
                'section':  block.heading_path,    # 'Notes > 14 Dividends'
                'page':     block.page,
            })

    # 3. Figures: a VLM writes the description that gets indexed
    for figure in images:
        description = vlm.describe(
            image=figure.data,
            prompt=(
                'Describe this figure from an annual report. State the '
                'chart type, axes and units, every series plotted, the '
                'direction of each series, and any printed values. '
                'Do not speculate beyond what is shown.'
            ),
        )
        chunks.append({
            'text':      description,
            'modality':  'chart' if figure.is_chart else 'image',
            'section':   figure.heading_path,
            'page':      figure.page,
            'image_ref': figure.uri,        # answer can link the original
        })

    # 4. Stamp the metadata that makes year-on-year questions possible
    for c in chunks:
        c['year'] = year
        c['report_type'] = report_type
        c['chunk_id'] = make_chunk_id(year, c['section'], c['page'])

    bm25_index.add(chunks)          # lexical
    vector_store.add(chunks)        # dense, embedded per chunk
    return len(chunks)
02 · Query Transformation

HyDE: embedding an answer instead of a question

There is a vocabulary gap at the heart of financial RAG. An analyst types eight words in plain English. The document answers in three hundred words of standardised accounting language. Embedding the eight words and hoping they land near the three hundred is optimistic.

The architecture uses HyDE — Hypothetical Document Embeddings — to close that gap. Before anything is retrieved, a small, fast model is asked to write the answer it imagines exists. That hypothetical document is almost certainly wrong on the facts, and that does not matter: it is never shown to anyone. It is used purely as the embedding input, because a passage written in the register of an annual report sits much closer, in vector space, to the real passage than the original question does.

The trick, in one line Retrieval is a similarity problem between two documents. So turn the question into a document first, then compare like with like.

Why it works on filings specifically

Annual reports are unusually formulaic. "Dividend", "payout ratio", "profit after tax", "the Board declared a final dividend of" — these phrases recur, near-verbatim, across two decades of filings. A hypothetical answer generated by an LLM reproduces that register almost by reflex, which means it carries far more anchor terms into the embedding than the question ever could.

The original question is not discarded. It keeps its own job: it drives the lexical branch of retrieval, where exact tokens — a year, a note number, a line-item name — matter more than semantics. The hypothetical document drives the dense branch. Each branch gets the input it is good at.

hyde.py
# ── HyDE: write the answer you wish existed, then embed that ─────────
from langchain_core.prompts import ChatPromptTemplate

HYDE_PROMPT = ChatPromptTemplate.from_template(
    '''You are writing a short passage exactly as it would appear in a
    company's annual report. Answer the question below in 80-120 words,
    in the formal register of a filing: use standard accounting terms,
    name the relevant statement or note, and write in full sentences.

    Invented figures are acceptable — this passage is used only as a
    retrieval probe and is never shown to a user. Do not hedge, do not
    say you lack information, and do not mention that this is
    hypothetical.

    Question: {question}
    Passage:'''
)

# A small, fast model: this runs on every query, so latency matters
hyde_llm = ChatOpenAI(model='gpt-4o-mini', temperature=0.0)
hyde_chain = HYDE_PROMPT | hyde_llm | StrOutputParser()


def transform_query(question: str, history: list) -> dict:
    '''Resolve references, then build the retrieval probe.'''
    # 'and the year after?' only means something with the thread attached
    standalone = resolve_with_history(question, history)

    hypothetical = hyde_chain.invoke({'question': standalone})

    return {
        'lexical_query': standalone,                   # feeds BM25
        'dense_vector':  embedder.embed(hypothetical), # feeds vectors
        'filters':       extract_filters(standalone),  # year, report type
        'hypothetical':  hypothetical,                 # kept for tracing
    }


def extract_filters(q: str) -> dict:
    '''Pull structured constraints out of the question so retrieval can
    narrow 20+ years of filings before it ranks anything.'''
    years = sorted(set(int(y) for y in YEAR_RE.findall(q)))
    return {
        'year': {'in': years} if years else None,
        'report_type': 'annual' if 'annual' in q.lower() else None,
    }
The honest caveat HyDE costs an extra LLM call on every query, and a hypothetical passage can drift off-topic for questions the archive genuinely does not cover. Pinning the generator to temperature zero, keeping it short, and always running the lexical branch off the real question are what keep that drift from reaching the answer.
03 · Retrieval

Hybrid search, reciprocal rank fusion, then a reranker

No single retrieval method is good at everything a finance question needs. Lexical search nails exact identifiers and fails on paraphrase; vector search understands paraphrase and drifts on rare tokens. So both run, on every query, in parallel.

BM25 only Insufficient

Excellent for "note 14", "segment reporting", a specific line-item name or a year. Blind to "how did management justify the cut?" when the report says "the Board considered it prudent to rebase the distribution".

Dense vectors only Insufficient

Handles paraphrase and concept matching well. But across twenty near-identical filings it happily returns the right passage from the wrong year, and it under-weights rare exact tokens.

Both, fused with RRF, then reranked Chosen

Each engine contributes its own ranked list; fusion rewards chunks that both agree on without ever comparing their incomparable raw scores; a cross-encoder then does the expensive, accurate ordering on the short list that survives.

Metadata filtering comes first

Before either engine ranks anything, the filters extracted during query transformation narrow the candidate pool by year, report type and section. With twenty-plus years of structurally identical documents, this is the single cheapest accuracy win in the pipeline — it removes the entire class of "right answer, wrong year" errors before ranking begins.

Why Reciprocal Rank Fusion rather than score blending

The obvious way to merge two result lists is to normalise the scores and take a weighted average. In practice that is fragile: a BM25 score and a cosine similarity live on different scales with different distributions, and the normalisation constant you tune on one query set stops working on the next.

RRF ignores scores entirely and uses only rank position. Each document scores 1 / (k + rank) in every list it appears in, and those contributions are summed. With k = 60, the curve is steep at the top and flat further down, so a document ranked first in one list and second in the other beats a document ranked first in one list and absent from the other — which is exactly the behaviour you want.

Reranking: the expensive model, used sparingly

Bi-encoders embed the query and the chunk separately, which is what makes them fast enough to search millions of vectors — and also what limits their precision, because the two never see each other. A cross-encoder reads the query and the candidate chunk together in one forward pass and scores the pair directly. It is far more accurate and far too slow to run over a whole index, so it runs over roughly the top fifty fused candidates and returns the handful that actually go into the prompt.

retrieval.py
# ── Hybrid retrieval: two engines, one fused list, then a reranker ───
from concurrent.futures import ThreadPoolExecutor
from sentence_transformers import CrossEncoder

RRF_K = 60          # flattens the curve so rank 1 does not dominate
CANDIDATES = 50     # how many survive fusion and reach the reranker
TOP_K = 6           # how many reach the prompt

reranker = CrossEncoder('BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3')


def reciprocal_rank_fusion(ranked_lists, k=RRF_K):
    '''Fuse any number of ranked lists using rank position only.

    Raw BM25 scores and cosine similarities are not comparable, so RRF
    never looks at them: a document scores 1/(k + rank) in each list it
    appears in, and those contributions are summed.
    '''
    fused = {}
    provenance = {}
    for list_name, docs in ranked_lists.items():
        for rank, doc in enumerate(docs, start=1):
            fused[doc.chunk_id] = fused.get(doc.chunk_id, 0.0) + 1.0 / (k + rank)
            provenance.setdefault(doc.chunk_id, []).append((list_name, rank))
    ordered = sorted(fused.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1], reverse=True)
    return [(cid, score, provenance[cid]) for cid, score in ordered]


def retrieve(question: str, history: list, top_k: int = TOP_K):
    probe = transform_query(question, history)     # HyDE happens here

    # Both branches run against the same metadata-filtered pool
    with ThreadPoolExecutor(max_workers=2) as pool:
        lexical = pool.submit(
            bm25_index.search,
            query=probe['lexical_query'],
            filters=probe['filters'],
            limit=CANDIDATES,
        )
        dense = pool.submit(
            vector_store.search,
            vector=probe['dense_vector'],
            filters=probe['filters'],
            limit=CANDIDATES,
        )

    fused = reciprocal_rank_fusion({
        'bm25':   lexical.result(),
        'vector': dense.result(),
    })[:CANDIDATES]

    # Cross-encoder: query and chunk read together, one pair at a time
    chunks = store.get_many([cid for cid, _, _ in fused])
    pairs = [(question, c.text) for c in chunks]
    scores = reranker.predict(pairs)

    ranked = sorted(zip(chunks, scores), key=lambda p: p[1], reverse=True)
    return [chunk for chunk, _ in ranked[:top_k]]
What the fusion step actually protects you from A question mentioning a note number will send BM25 straight to the right note while the vector branch wanders. A question phrased entirely in the analyst's own words will do the reverse. RRF means the pipeline does not have to know in advance which kind of question it just received.
04 · Memory

Two tiers of memory, because research is a conversation

Nobody researches a company in one question. They ask something, read the answer, and then ask "and how did that change the year after?" — a sentence that is meaningless without everything that came before it.

Short-term: the session

Short-term memory is the live message history for the current thread. It is what lets query transformation resolve "that", "the year after" and "the same segment" into a standalone question before retrieval ever runs — which matters doubly here, because a HyDE probe generated from an unresolved pronoun retrieves nothing useful.

Long-term: Mem0, backed by PostgreSQL

Long-term memory uses Mem0, which does something more selective than storing transcripts: it extracts the durable facts from a conversation, deduplicates them against what it already holds, and recalls the relevant ones on later turns. For this system that means remembering which companies and years an analyst works on, which metrics they keep coming back to, and how they like answers framed.

PostgreSQL is the checkpointer store underneath both tiers — thread state and extracted memories are durable rows, not process memory. A restarted service does not lose a conversation, and a Monday morning session can pick up where Friday afternoon left off.

memory.py
# ── Two tiers: session state and durable memories, both on Postgres ──
from mem0 import Memory
from langgraph.checkpoint.postgres import PostgresSaver

# Short-term: LangGraph thread state, persisted rather than in-process
checkpointer = PostgresSaver.from_conn_string(settings.PG_DSN)
checkpointer.setup()

# Long-term: Mem0 extracts and recalls facts, with Postgres underneath
memory = Memory.from_config({
    'vector_store': {
        'provider': 'pgvector',
        'config': {
            'connection_string': settings.PG_DSN,
            'collection_name': 'fingpt_memories',
        },
    },
    'llm': {'provider': 'openai', 'config': {'model': 'gpt-4o-mini'}},
})


def answer_turn(question: str, user_id: str, thread_id: str) -> str:
    config = {'configurable': {'thread_id': thread_id}}

    # Long-term recall: only the memories relevant to this question
    recalled = memory.search(query=question, user_id=user_id, limit=5)
    preamble = '\n'.join('- ' + m['memory'] for m in recalled['results'])

    result = graph.invoke(
        {
            'messages': [HumanMessage(content=question)],
            'user_context': preamble,      # 'covers the 2009-2019 window'
        },
        config=config,                     # short-term restored by thread
    )

    reply = result['messages'][-1].content

    # Write back: Mem0 decides what is worth keeping, and dedupes it
    memory.add(
        messages=[
            {'role': 'user', 'content': question},
            {'role': 'assistant', 'content': reply},
        ],
        user_id=user_id,
        metadata={'thread_id': thread_id, 'source': 'fingpt'},
    )
    return reply
Why not just replay the whole transcript? Because it does not scale and it does not generalise. A transcript grows without bound and buries the useful facts in filler; an extracted memory store keeps a short, deduplicated set of statements that are actually worth carrying into the next session — and can be inspected and corrected.
05 · Answers

Generation that shows its working

Everything before this point exists to make the final prompt small, relevant and attributable. Generation reads only the reranked top-k chunks, and every chunk arrives with the metadata it was indexed with — year, report, section path, page and modality — so the model can cite precisely rather than gesture at "the annual report".

The rules the generator works under

  • Answer only from the supplied context — no background knowledge about the company, no filling gaps from the model's own priors
  • Attach a chunk id to every figure, so an assertion can be traced to a page
  • Name the year on every number, because in a twenty-year archive an unlabelled figure is a liability
  • Reproduce table values verbatim rather than restating them from memory
  • Say when the archive is silent — an explicit "the filings do not disclose this" is a valid and useful answer
  • Mark chart-derived values as chart-derived, since a VLM description of a graph is an interpretation, not a disclosed figure

A question, end to end

Step 1Resolve
Short-term memory rewrites "and the year after?" into a standalone question using the previous turn
Step 2Transform
HyDE writes a ~100-word hypothetical filing passage; filters year in (2012) are extracted from the text
Step 3Retrieve
BM25 on the question and vector search on the hypothetical run in parallel over the filtered pool
Step 4Fuse
reciprocal_rank_fusion() merges both lists on rank position and keeps the top candidates
Step 5Rerank
The cross-encoder reads each (question, chunk) pair and returns the final top-k — here a table chunk and a chart description
Step 6Generate
The answer is composed from those chunks only, with [chunk_id] citations and the year stated on every figure
Step 7Remember
Mem0 extracts what is durable from the exchange; thread state is checkpointed to PostgreSQL
generate.py
# ── Grounded generation with resolvable citations ────────────────────
ANSWER_PROMPT = '''You are a financial research assistant working over a
company's annual-report archive. Answer using ONLY the context below.

RULES
  1. Cite the chunk id in square brackets after every figure or claim.
  2. State the reporting year alongside every number.
  3. Quote table values exactly as they appear; never recompute them.
  4. If a value comes from a chart description, say it was read from a
     chart rather than disclosed in a table.
  5. If the context does not answer the question, say so plainly and
     name what would be needed. Do not fill the gap from memory.

WHAT THIS ANALYST USUALLY CARES ABOUT
{user_context}

CONTEXT
{context}

QUESTION
{question}
'''


def build_context(chunks) -> str:
    '''Metadata travels with the text, so citations can resolve.'''
    blocks = []
    for c in chunks:
        blocks.append(
            '[{id}] FY{year} | {section} | p.{page} | {modality}\n{text}'.format(
                id=c.chunk_id, year=c.year, section=c.section,
                page=c.page, modality=c.modality, text=c.text,
            )
        )
    return '\n\n'.join(blocks)


def generate(question, chunks, user_context=''):
    answer = answer_llm.invoke(ANSWER_PROMPT.format(
        question=question,
        context=build_context(chunks),
        user_context=user_context or 'no stored preferences yet',
    )).content

    # Every [id] in the answer must map to a chunk that was actually
    # supplied — an unresolvable citation is a generation failure.
    cited = set(CITATION_RE.findall(answer))
    supplied = {c.chunk_id for c in chunks}
    unknown = cited - supplied
    if unknown:
        log.warning('unresolvable citations: %s', unknown)

    return {
        'answer': answer,
        'sources': [c.as_source() for c in chunks if c.chunk_id in cited],
    }

What the architecture buys

Tables and charts are first-class

A figure only published as a bar chart is still findable, because it was described in words at ingestion time and indexed like any other chunk.

Right passage, right year

Metadata filtering runs before ranking, so twenty structurally identical documents stop competing with each other for the same query.

Robust to how the question is phrased

HyDE closes the register gap, hybrid search covers both exact and conceptual matching, and RRF means neither branch has to win outright.

A conversation, not a search box

Session memory resolves follow-ups; Mem0 on PostgreSQL carries what matters into next week's session rather than starting cold.

What I would improve next

  • Table question-answering as its own path — for questions that are really arithmetic over a retrieved table, executing a query against structured rows is more reliable than asking a language model to read the grid
  • A held-out evaluation set of analyst questions, scored on retrieval recall and citation correctness, so changes to chunking or to the reranker can be measured rather than argued about
  • Verifying chart-derived values against any nearby table that publishes the same series, and flagging disagreement in the answer instead of silently preferring one
  • Tuning the RRF weighting per query type — identifier-heavy questions could lean further towards the lexical list than conceptual ones do
  • Caching HyDE probes for recurring question shapes, to take the extra generation call off the hot path
  • Incremental re-ingestion, so a newly published filing joins the index without a full rebuild of two decades of documents
