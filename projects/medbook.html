The Problem

Three ways to book the same doctor, and one way to get it wrong

A clinic takes bookings on the phone, through its website, and across the front desk. Each of those is a separate queue with its own idea of who is free. The failure mode is boring and constant: two people are booked into the same fifteen minutes, and someone finds out in the waiting room.

I wanted to see whether the phone line could stop being the slow, human-shaped path — whether a caller could describe a symptom in their own words and come away with a real appointment, while the front desk watched it appear on their screen. That meant three things had to be true at once: the agent must only ever speak facts that came out of the database, the database must make double-booking impossible rather than unlikely, and every screen in the building must agree within milliseconds.

Talking to it like a receptionist

Caller

“I've had an itchy red rash on my arms for a week.”

Agent

“That sounds like one for dermatology. Dr. Michael Chen treats acne and eczema, and he's free on Thursday at ten past ten. Shall I book that?”

The agent understands the symptom, maps it to the right department, checks live availability, takes a phone number, and writes the booking — in one continuous conversation, with no menus and no keypad input. Patients who prefer a screen get the same availability on the public site, and staff get a dashboard with one column per doctor, every slot marked free or booked and labelled with where the booking came from: voice agent, website, or reception.

The hard constraint A receptionist who invents a doctor is worse than no receptionist. Every name, time, room and price the agent says out loud has to be traceable to a tool call made in that same conversation.

From a sentence to a specialist

Callers do not say “Cardiology”. They say “a heart doctor”, or describe a symptom, or the speech model mishears them entirely. Matching is done in SQL — trigram similarity over department and doctor names, plus a keyword array holding symptoms, job titles and common misspellings — so cardiologist, heart doctor and the mishearing cardiologie all land on the same department.

01 · Architecture

Three services, one database, no second source of truth

The system is deliberately boring in shape: a voice agent, a web service, and PostgreSQL. What makes it work is that none of the three carries its own copy of the rules. The agent does not know when a doctor is free — it asks. The website does not decide whether a slot is bookable — the database does. Every booking path, however it started, converges on the same constraints and the same functions.

  • Voice agent (Python) — LiveKit Agents runs the session and turn-taking, Gemini Live handles speech in and speech out, and 12 function tools are the only way the model can touch data
  • Web service (FastAPI + React) — one SPA serving the public site and the admin area, 24 REST endpoints behind it, and a WebSocket hub pushing live updates
  • PostgreSQL — 6 tables, 5 SQL functions including available_slots() and match_specializations(), and a trigger that announces every change
Why the agent gets no shortcuts The voice agent talks to PostgreSQL through the same functions and constraints the website uses. There is no “agent fast path”, which means a bug in the agent can produce a bad conversation but not a bad calendar.
02 · A booking end to end

How a spoken sentence becomes a row, and then a pixel

The interesting part of a voice booking is not the speech. It is everything that has to be true between “yes, book it” and the moment a receptionist across the building sees the slot fill in without touching anything.

Below is a full call, from the caller's first sentence to the confirmation. Two details are worth watching: the agent collects a phone number before it writes anything, so a booking always belongs to an identifiable patient; and the notification that reaches the staff calendar comes from the database itself, not from the agent.

The same shape holds for a cancellation. The agent calls the cancel tool, the row's status changes, the trigger fires, and the freed slot reappears on the staff grid just as fast.

03 · Data model

Six tables, and the one thing that deliberately isn't a table

The schema is small on purpose. Specializations own doctors; doctors own a weekly schedule and a leave calendar; patients and doctors meet in appointments. Every appointment records booked_via, so the front desk can always see whether a slot came from the phone line, the website or their own colleague.

Availability as a function, not a record

The obvious design is a slots table that gets pre-generated and then marked as taken. It is also the design that eventually shows a slot that no longer exists, because something wrote to the schedule, the leave calendar or the appointments table without regenerating it. Deriving availability in SQL means the question “is Dr. Khan free on Wednesday at nine?” is answered from the same three sources every time, by everyone.

The voice tools are thin wrappers over exactly that. A tool takes plain arguments, calls a function, and returns structured facts — it never composes a sentence, because the sentence is the model's job and the facts are the database's.

agent/tools.py
# ── A LiveKit function tool: plain arguments, structured facts ───────
from livekit.agents import function_tool, RunContext


@function_tool()
async def search_doctors(
    context: RunContext,
    query: str,
    preferred_date: str | None = None,
) -> dict:
    '''Find doctors for a symptom, a department or a job title.

    Use for anything the caller says about who they want to see:
    "a heart doctor", "cardiologist", "an itchy rash".
    Never answer from memory - always call this first.
    '''
    async with db.acquire() as conn:
        # trigram + keyword match, ranked; returns real departments
        depts = await conn.fetch(
            'SELECT * FROM match_specializations($1) LIMIT 3', query
        )
        if not depts:
            # never a bare error: tell the agent what the clinic HAS
            return {'matched': False, 'departments': await list_departments(conn)}

        doctors = await conn.fetch(
            '''SELECT d.id, d.full_name, s.name AS department,
                      d.qualifications, d.consultation_fee
                 FROM doctors d
                 JOIN specializations s ON s.id = d.specialization_id
                WHERE s.id = ANY($1::int[])''',
            [d['id'] for d in depts]
        )

        # next free slot per doctor, derived - never read from a table
        for doc in doctors:
            doc['next_slots'] = await conn.fetch(
                'SELECT * FROM available_slots($1, $2) LIMIT 3',
                doc['id'], preferred_date or date.today()
            )

    return {'matched': True, 'doctors': [dict(d) for d in doctors]}
04 · Booking integrity

Double-booking is impossible, not merely unlikely

Three independent writers — the phone, the website and the front desk — can target the same slot in the same millisecond. Guarding that in application code means writing the same check three times and hoping all three stay correct forever.

So the rule does not live in application code. It lives in PostgreSQL as an exclusion constraint: no two scheduled appointments for the same doctor may have overlapping time ranges. The database is not asked to be polite about it; the second writer simply cannot commit.

db/schema.sql
CONSTRAINT no_doctor_overlap EXCLUDE USING gist (
    doctor_id WITH =,
    tsrange(starts_at, ends_at) WITH &&
) WHERE (status = 'scheduled')

A second constraint of the same shape stops one patient being in two places at once. Losing writers get a clean, specific error rather than a corrupted calendar — and the agent turns that error into the only sensible sentence: “That slot was just taken — the next one is at half past.”

Proven by test, not assumed Two concurrent booking attempts on the same slot were run against real Postgres. One commits, one is rejected, and the rejected caller is offered another time. The constraint is the test's subject, not its scaffolding.

Why the failure path matters more than the happy path

A booking tool that can only succeed produces an agent that cannot handle being wrong. Every write tool returns the reason it failed in a form the model can act on — slot taken, doctor on leave, time in the past, appointment belongs to someone else — so the conversation continues instead of stalling on an apology. The tests cover exactly those cases: double booking, booking in the past, and cancelling an appointment that is not yours.

05 · Anti-hallucination

The time the agent invented a doctor

An early version told a caller “we have no cardiologist”. When the caller pushed back, it invented a doctor's name — and then read another doctor's real availability under it.

That is the worst possible failure for this product, and it was not one bug. It was a chain of three, each harmless on its own. A brittle search never matched the word “cardiologist” to the department “Cardiology”. The failed lookup returned an error carrying no facts at all. And the department listing tool returned department names without the doctors inside them. By the time the model was pressed for a name, nothing in its context contradicted making one up.

The rule that does the work A failed search must never return a bare error. It returns what the clinic does have — real departments, real doctors — so the model's next best move is to offer a real alternative rather than to fill the silence.

The regression test is the incident itself, replayed: the judge scenario has the caller assert that the invented doctor exists and demand a booking with them. The agent is expected to hold its ground and offer the real list instead.

06 · Realtime fan-out

Realtime that doesn't care who made the change

The tempting way to build a live calendar is to push an update from the API handler that just wrote the row. It works right up until a second writer appears — and here there are at least four.

So the live feed is driven by the database, not by handlers. An AFTER INSERT / UPDATE / DELETE trigger on appointments calls pg_notify; the API holds one long-lived LISTEN connection; and every notification is fanned out over WebSockets to every open tab. A booking made by voice, by the website, by reception, or by someone typing into psql produces exactly the same event.

How the 5 ms was measured A headless browser opens the staff calendar and waits. A row is inserted directly into Postgres, bypassing every service. The test asserts the DOM changes without a reload, and measures the gap between the write and the mutation: 5 ms. A cancellation frees the slot on screen just as fast.
07 · Model constraints

Working within a realtime model's real constraints

A realtime speech model is not a text model with audio bolted on. Building on Gemini Live surfaced four limits that only appear under a real call, and none of them announced themselves as errors — they showed up as a conversation behaving strangely.

Each one was found the same way: read the logs, then read the provider's source, rather than guessing at prompt changes. The fixes are all one line.

SymptomCauseFix
Replies arrived a turn late, or never Client-side turn detection, which Gemini Live doesn't support — commit_audio is a no-op turn_detection="realtime_llm"
The agent read its own plan aloud Gemini streams private reasoning as transcript by default include_thoughts=False
The session restarted five times per call Every change to the tool list restarts a Gemini session Stop hiding a tool during the greeting
The call froze mid-booking AgentTask inside a function tool is unsupported — manual_function_calls=False Plain tool arguments instead
What it bought With turn-taking handed to the model and the session no longer restarting mid-call, the measured gap from a tool completing to the agent speaking again is 0.65 s — close enough to a human pause that callers stop noticing it.

The lesson that generalises

Three of the four problems came from applying a text-agent habit to a realtime session: manage the turn yourself, hide tools until you need them, spawn a sub-task inside a tool. All three are reasonable patterns elsewhere and all three are actively harmful here. The realtime session is a stateful thing you configure once and then leave alone.

agent/session.py
# ── One session, configured once, tool list never changes ────────────
session = AgentSession(
    llm=google.beta.realtime.RealtimeModel(
        model='gemini-3.1-flash-live-preview',
        voice='Aoede',
        # the model owns turn-taking; client-side commit_audio is a no-op
        turn_detection='realtime_llm',
        # otherwise the agent reads its private reasoning out loud
        include_thoughts=False,
    ),
)

# All 12 tools registered up front. Adding or removing one mid-call
# restarts the Gemini session and drops the conversation.
await session.start(
    agent=Receptionist(tools=ALL_TOOLS),
    room=ctx.room,
)
08 · Testing

Three layers, because three things can be wrong

A voice booking system can fail as a conversation, as a database, or as a live interface — and a test that covers one tells you nothing about the other two. So the suite is split along those lines.

Conversation tests LLM judge

An LLM judges whether the agent recommends the right specialist for a described symptom, redirects an emergency instead of booking it, asks for a phone number before writing anything, and refuses to invent a doctor even when the caller insists one exists.

Database tests Real Postgres

Every tool exercised against a real database, with the failure paths as first-class cases: double booking the same slot, booking in the past, and cancelling an appointment that belongs to someone else.

Browser automation Playwright

Playwright books through the public site, signs into the admin area, then writes to the database directly and asserts the open calendar updates without a reload — which is the only honest way to test the realtime path.

Why the browser test bypasses the API If the test booked through the API, it would prove that the API can push an update. Writing straight to Postgres proves the thing that actually matters: the calendar reacts to the data changing, no matter who changed it.

09 · Limits & production

What this is, and what it isn't

This was built as a demonstration. Every doctor, patient and phone number in it is fictional, and it has never seen real patient data — which is the only reason it is honest to publish a case study about it at all.

The engineering that makes bookings safe is real: the constraints, the derived availability, the notification path and the grounding rules would all survive contact with a clinic. The things that make patient data safe are deliberately out of scope, and putting this in front of real patients would mean building them first.

  • Per-user staff accounts — the admin area is a single shared login; real use needs named accounts with roles, so an action can be attributed to a person
  • Encryption in transit and at rest — including the call audio and any transcript retained from it
  • An audit trail — who read or changed which patient record, and when, kept separately from the records themselves
  • Retention policies — how long recordings, transcripts and cancelled appointments are kept, and what deletes them
  • The regulatory work — HIPAA or UK GDPR depending on where it runs, which is a programme rather than a feature
Clinical scope, stated plainly The agent gives no medical advice. It maps what a caller describes to a department and a named doctor, and anything that sounds like an emergency is referred to emergency services rather than booked.

What I'd build next

  • Outbound reminder calls on the same agent, reusing the booking tools in reverse — confirm, reschedule or cancel
  • A waiting list that watches the notification channel and offers a freed slot to the next caller automatically
  • Multi-language handling, since the matching layer is already phrase-tolerant and the department keywords are just arrays

Correctness in one place

Overlap rules and availability live in the database, so three independent writers cannot disagree about who is free.

Grounded by construction

Tools return facts, never prose, and a failed lookup returns real alternatives — so there is nothing to invent.

Live without coupling

The push path is driven by a trigger, so a new writer gets realtime updates without a line of frontend code.

A conversation that recovers

Every write tool reports why it failed in terms the model can act on, so a lost race becomes another offered time.
